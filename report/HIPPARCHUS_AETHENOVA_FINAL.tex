\begin{abstract}
SPHEREx images the entire sky repeatedly, so any star can in principle be compared across epochs. Doing this
naively gives false results: the linear variable filters put the same star at a different wavelength in every
visit, bright stars saturate, and zodiacal light changes with the season. HIPPARCHUS is an open tool that finds
every SPHEREx exposure of a position in the NASA/IPAC IRSA archive, checks each one, and compares only pairs that
are scientifically compatible, refusing the others with a stated reason. On real DR1 and QR3 data it finds
8 valid comparisons out of 1540 possible pairs for RW~Aurigae in detector~1. In all of them the star is brighter in the later
epoch, by up to a factor of $2.56$ over 371 days ($175\sigma$ formal), while a control star in the same images stays
constant within $2.2\sigma$. For Barnard's Star it rejects all 58 epochs as saturated and refuses to compare. For the faint
brown dwarf WISE~0855$-$0714 it finds 27 valid pairs and no significant change, with a scatter of significances
(0.83) that shows the error model is not underestimated. All inputs, checksums, settings and outputs are recorded.
HIPPARCHUS has not found Planet X and does not claim to; it builds the validated multi-epoch comparison that any
search for a slowly moving distant planet in SPHEREx data would first need.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The Planet X and SPHEREx challenge asks how SPHEREx data can reveal things that change or move in the sky.
SPHEREx \parencite{dore2014} observes every point of the sky several times a year in 102 spectral channels between
about 0.75 and \SI{5}{\micro\meter}, using linear variable filters (LVFs): the wavelength seen by a pixel depends on
where it lies on the detector. A star is therefore observed at a different wavelength in each visit. Comparing two
raw images of the same star and calling the difference ``variability'' is wrong unless the wavelengths agree.

HIPPARCHUS (named after the astronomer who found that stars change and move by comparing his catalogue with older
ones) is built around one idea: \emph{a comparison is only reported when it is valid, and otherwise refused with a
reason a person can check}. Refusal is a result, not a failure.
Section~\ref{sec:planetx} explains how this connects to the search for a distant planet, and
Section~\ref{sec:status} separates what is validated from what is experimental or not demonstrated.

\section{Scientific context: Planet X / Planet Nine}\label{sec:planetx}
\paragraph{The hypothesis.} \textcite{trujillo2014} found 2012~VP$_{113}$, a Sedna-like body with a perihelion of
80\,AU, and noticed that the most distant known trans-Neptunian objects have similar arguments of perihelion; they
suggested that an unseen massive body could be shaping their orbits. \textcite{batygin2016} showed that the orbits of
these objects are also clustered in physical space and proposed a distant giant planet on an eccentric orbit
(``Planet Nine'') as the explanation. The planet has not been observed. The challenge name, ``Planet X'', refers to
this kind of undiscovered distant planet.

\paragraph{What it would look like to SPHEREx.} A planet hundreds of AU away moves slowly across the sky, and most of
that motion is not its own. Its orbital speed scales as $r^{-1/2}$, so at $r=500$\,AU it is about $1.3$\,km\,s$^{-1}$,
which is about $0.3''$ per day seen from that distance. The dominant apparent motion is the reflex of the Earth's own
orbital motion (parallax): with $v_\oplus\approx29.8$\,km\,s$^{-1}$ the largest apparent rate, near opposition, is
\begin{equation*}
\dot\theta \approx \frac{v_\oplus}{d} \approx 7''\,\mathrm{day}^{-1}\ \text{at}\ d=500\,\mathrm{AU}
\qquad(12''\,\mathrm{day}^{-1}\ \text{at}\ 300\,\mathrm{AU},\ 3.5''\,\mathrm{day}^{-1}\ \text{at}\ 1000\,\mathrm{AU}),
\end{equation*}
that is about $0.1'$ per day, or roughly one $6.15''$ SPHEREx pixel per day at 500\,AU. Over a year the parallax traces
an ellipse with a semi-major axis of $1/d$ radians, about $410''$ (67 pixels) at 500\,AU. So the object is effectively
fixed within one exposure, moves by a few pixels between visits a few days apart, and moves far outside one of our
$0.05^\circ$ ($\approx180''$) cutouts between the six-month visits that HIPPARCHUS compares. A fixed-position comparison like
ours sees it, at best, as a source present in one epoch and absent in the other, not as a resolved track.

It would also be faint. Reflected sunlight falls as $r^{-2}$ on the way out and $\Delta^{-2}$ on the way back, so roughly
as $r^{-4}$ for a distant body; the equilibrium temperature falls as $r^{-1/2}$, to about 12\,K at 500\,AU without
internal heat, so the thermal emission peaks far beyond the \SI{5}{\micro\meter} limit of SPHEREx and the detector would
mostly see reflected light. Any candidate would therefore sit near the single-epoch detection limit, which is exactly
the regime where our WISE~0855 analysis (median $S/N\approx2.7$ per epoch) shows that a single cutout cannot say much.

\paragraph{Why multi-epoch validation comes first.} Any moving-object search in SPHEREx data compares epochs: a source
seen in one visit and missing, or displaced, in another. Every artefact HIPPARCHUS refuses would also look like such a
source: the linear variable filters change the wavelength between visits, saturated stars bleed and leave persistence,
transients and ghosts appear in one exposure only, and the zodiacal light changes with the season. A search that does
not first match wavelengths, honour the pipeline flags, measure its own false-alarm rate with null tests, and know its
completeness from injection-recovery would produce candidates that cannot be told apart from artefacts. This report
builds and tests that comparison step on fixed targets, where the right answer is known or checkable.

\paragraph{What this work does not claim.} \textbf{HIPPARCHUS has not found Planet X}, and it has not searched for it.
Our analysis covers three fixed targets in small cutouts. A non-detection in data like ours would not rule the planet
out: the predicted position is uncertain over a large region of sky, the planet would move out of a fixed cutout between
epochs, and it may well be fainter than what a single SPHEREx epoch can detect.

\section{Data}
All images come from the IRSA image search service (IVOA SIA~v2, \url{https://irsa.ipac.caltech.edu/SIA}) for two
collections: SPHEREx DR1 spectral images \parencite{irsa_dr1} and Quick Release QR3 \parencite{irsa_qr3}. When an
exposure is in both, the DR1 version is used. Only \texttt{science} products are kept. For each exposure we request a
$0.05^\circ$ server-side cutout (about $29\times29$ pixels of $6.15''$) and store it with its SHA-256 checksum; the
checksum is verified every time a cached file is reused. Each cutout contains the calibrated image (MJy\,sr$^{-1}$),
the pipeline flags, the variance, the zodiacal-light model and a spectral WCS lookup table.

centre the cutouts; the star is measured in the images. The main CDS servers were offline after a cybersecurity

SIMBAD \parencite{simbad2000} and Gaia DR3 \parencite{gaiadr3} through the mirrors CDS recommends (Section~\ref{sec:val}).

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\paragraph{Per-epoch checks.} For each cutout we (1) subtract the pipeline's zodiacal-light model, since zodiacal
light changes with the season; (2) locate the target with the celestial WCS (TAN-SIP); (3) read the wavelength and
channel width \emph{at the target pixel} from the spectral lookup WCS; and (4) reject the epoch if the target lies
within the aperture radius of the cutout edge, if any pixel within $r=2$\,px carries a rejecting flag (OVERFLOW,
NONLINEAR, TRANSIENT, SUR\_ERROR, PERSIST, BLOOM, GHOST, HOT, and others; 18 planes in total, read by name from each
file's own FLAGS header), if values are missing, or if the variance is not positive. SOURCE is not a rejecting flag,
since the target is itself a source; DICHROIC and PHANMISS are reported as warnings.

\paragraph{Pair compatibility.} Two epochs are compared only if both are usable, come from the same detector, and
the target's wavelengths differ by at most half a channel width. Pairs taken less than 0.5 days apart are treated as
null tests of the noise.

\paragraph{What the half-channel rule means in light collected.} We checked the rule against the spectral response it
stands for (\texttt{reproduce\_compat.py}, \texttt{compat\_check.json}). We model each exposure's response at the
star as one channel centred on the wavelength at the star, with the channel width as full width, and try two shapes:
a top-hat, and a Gaussian with FWHM equal to the width. We did not use the measured per-pixel response curves
\parencite{hui2026}; the two shapes bracket the answer. At the rule's limit two equal channels share exactly 50\% (top-hat) or 55.6\% (Gaussian) of their response,
so the rule is the same as asking for a top-hat overlap of at least one half; over all 4,183 pairs of the three
targets the two give identical verdicts. No published pair has an overlap below 0.61 (RW~Aur) or 0.50 (WISE~0855,
top-hat; 0.56 Gaussian). Among usable pairs refused only for wavelength (145 for RW~Aur, 639 for WISE~0855), none
reaches an overlap of 0.7 under either shape, and only 3 WISE~0855 pairs reach 0.5 under the Gaussian shape; the rule
does not throw away pairs that see clearly the same light.

\paragraph{Compatibility graph.} Per target we also store the pairs as a graph: epochs are nodes, compared pairs are
edges with $\Delta\lambda$, overlap, ratio and significance, and every refused pair keeps its reason codes
(Fig.~\ref{fig:compat}). Rebuilt from the epoch records alone, the graph gives back exactly the published 8 (RW~Aur),
27 (WISE~0855) and 0 (Barnard's Star) pairs and the published refusal counts. The RW~Aur edges form 2 groups (largest
8 epochs); WISE~0855 has 11 groups (largest 5): its pairs are short chains at fixed wavelength, not one connected web.

\paragraph{Comparison.} The later image, its variance and its flag mask are reprojected onto the earlier epoch's
pixel grid (\texttt{reproject}, bilinear). Flux is the sum inside $r=2$\,px minus the median sky in a 4--7\,px
annulus; its uncertainty combines the pixel variances and the error of the sky level. A change is called significant
at $|\Delta F|/\sigma_{\Delta F}\ge5$. We also report the flux ratio, a per-pixel significance map, and the shift of the
light-weighted centroid in arcseconds. As a check of calibration, the brightest clean local peak at least 9\,px from
the target is measured the same way in the same two images (\emph{control star}).

\section{Validation}\label{sec:val}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Unit tests} (35, \texttt{pytest}) run on real SPHEREx cutouts committed as fixtures, with their source
URL and checksum recorded. They cover cache integrity, flag decoding from headers, refusal of files that lack a
required extension, targets near RA~$0^\circ$, wavelength at the target, refusal of wavelength mismatches and flagged
epochs, the classification of same-visit pairs, a self-comparison that must show no change ($|\sigma|<0.5$ after
reprojection), the control-star check, the evidence graph, proper-motion propagation and catalogue matching, and that a partial archive answer never replaces a
complete saved listing.
\item \textbf{Control stars.} For the six RW~Aur pairs with a control star, its ratio lies between 0.86 and 1.07 and
its significance between $-2.2\sigma$ and $+0.7\sigma$ (Table~\ref{tab:rw}).
\item \textbf{Error model.} For WISE~0855, where the target is barely detected (median $S/N\approx2.7$ per epoch), the
27 pair significances have a standard deviation of 0.83 and a maximum of $|1.9|\sigma$. They behave like noise, so
the formal errors are not underestimated at low flux.
\item \textbf{Null test with blank apertures.} No same-visit pairs exist for these targets, so we run the same
photometry at a grid of positions away from the target in every valid pair (positions $5$\,px apart, clear of masked
pixels). Sky and ordinary stars should not change, so with correct errors the significances spread with standard
deviation 1. We find 1.08 for WISE~0855 ($n=55$), 0.55 for RW~Aur ($n=38$) and 0.91 overall ($n=93$), with no value
beyond $3\sigma$ (Figure~\ref{fig:val}, left). The errors are therefore right or conservative. For RW~Aur they are
too large by about a factor of two; Section~\ref{sec:rob} shows this is not caused by the reprojection.
\item \textbf{Injection-recovery (SIMULATED).} We add artificial Gaussian stars (FWHM from the image header) to the
later epoch at the same blank positions, with known $S/N$ inside the aperture, and rerun the comparison. These
sources are simulated, are kept in a separate section of \texttt{validation.json}, and are never mixed with real
measurements. The method recovers 44\% at $S/N=5$, 87\% at 6 and 99\% at 7 (Figure~\ref{fig:val}, right), as
expected for a $5\sigma$ threshold; nothing is recovered below $S/N=3$. The photon noise of the injected star is not
added, which makes bright injections slightly easier than real ones.
\item \textbf{Evidence graph.} Each result is linked to its epochs, cutouts (SHA-256), observations, archive queries
(SHA-256 of the raw answer) and the dataset DOI. For all three targets the graph has no broken links and no result
without a DOI (\texttt{submission/evidence/graph\_*.json}); a test checks that a broken link is detected.
\item \textbf{Catalogue match.} SIMBAD (CfA Harvard mirror) and Gaia DR3 (IRSA copy) were queried on
15~November~2026; both positions were moved to epoch 2026.0 with their proper motions.
The answers and their SHA-256 are kept in \texttt{catalog\_match.json}. All three presets fall on the right object: RW~Aur is
$2.1''$ (0.34~px) from SIMBAD, Barnard's Star $5.4''$ (0.88~px; it moves $10''$ per year), WISE~0855 $1.1''$
(0.19~px). The match also shows what lies inside our $12.3''$ aperture: for RW~Aur both components of the binary
(Gaia $G=13.0$ and $12.2$, $1.6''$ apart) and a $G=18.8$ star; for WISE~0855, which Gaia does not detect, a
$G=16.3$ field star $6.8''$ away.
\end{itemize}

\section{Results}
\paragraph{A. Valid comparison: RW Aurigae.} RW Aur is a young star with a history of deep dimming events caused by
its disk \parencite{rodriguez2013}. Of 1540 possible pairs, 1480 fail the wavelength test and 1387 involve a flagged
epoch (mostly OVERFLOW: RW Aur saturates detector~1 in 37 of 56 epochs). Eight pairs remain (Table~\ref{tab:rw}). In
all eight the star is brighter in the later epoch. The pairs chain consistently: $\times1.67$ from September 2025 to
March 2026, then $\times1.42$ to September 2026, giving $\approx\times2.4$ against $\times2.50$--$2.56$ measured directly. The
centroid moves by $<0.8''$ (an eighth of a pixel), so the light comes from the same star. Figure~\ref{fig:rw} shows the
strongest pair.

\paragraph{C. Insufficient signal: WISE 0855$-$0714.} The coldest known brown dwarf \parencite{luhman2014} is detected
only weakly in D6. HIPPARCHUS finds 27 valid pairs and no change above $1.9\sigma$. This does \emph{not} show the object

may come from that star.

RW~Aur the 2026 points lie above the 2025 points at the same wavelength, the same brightening seen in the pairs.

\section{How robust are the conclusions?}\label{sec:rob}
After the results above were fixed, we audited the measurement for nine possible problems and repeated it under
other reasonable analysis choices (code in \texttt{robustness.py}, results in
\texttt{robustness.json}). The production numbers did not move: the same pairs, ratios and significances as the
published evidence files, to the last digit.
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Bad pixels and missing sky.} The photometry used to fall back silently to a sky of zero when the annulus
was empty, and did not refuse a flagged pixel inside the aperture. Neither happened in our data, but both now return
no flux and the pair is refused (two new tests).
\item \textbf{Errors after reprojection.} A simulation of pure noise rotated by $37^\circ$ and shifted onto another
grid, as in our comparison, gives a scatter of the aperture flux of 0.99 times the predicted error, so aperture
errors are right. Single pixels were not: the per-pixel significance map used $\sum w\sigma^2$ instead of
$\sum w^2\sigma^2$ and its scatter was 0.56 of the prediction. With the correction it is 0.94. This changes only the
significance map, not any result. The excess spread for RW~Aur in the null test is therefore not caused by
reprojection; we do not yet know its origin.
\item \textbf{PSF width.} A star whose FWHM grows from 1.5 to 2.0\,px loses 7\% inside our 2\,px aperture (0.4\% at
3\,px). Within each of our pairs the header FWHM is the same (1.22\,px for RW~Aur, 1.47\,px for WISE~0855).
\item \textbf{Wavelength.} Our pairs differ by 0.5--6.8\,nm, against half-channel limits of 8.5--11\,nm, which the
band-centre accuracy of \textcite{hui2026} supports. With the rule tightened to 0.25 or loosened to 0.75 of a channel
(2, 8, 12 RW~Aur pairs; 11, 27, 38 WISE~0855 pairs) every conclusion still holds.
\item \textbf{Calibration between epochs.} Instead of one control star we also use every clean, isolated field star at
$S/N\ge10$ in the cutout. Our $29\times29$\,px cutouts hold only 0--2 such stars, so this ensemble is small. Its scatter
gives a calibration floor of 12.5\% for RW~Aur. Added to the formal errors, the RW~Aur significances become 0.7--12.4$\sigma$
instead of up to $175\sigma$, and the target still changes by 1.5--3.0 times relative to the field stars.
\item \textbf{Many tests.} With 8 (RW~Aur) or 27 (WISE~0855) pairs, the chance of at least one false $5\sigma$ event
is $5\times10^{-6}$ or $1.5\times10^{-5}$; keeping the single-test rate would need $5.4\sigma$ or $5.6\sigma$.
The RW~Aur detection passes both.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Stability.} Each claim was rechecked with aperture radii 1.5--3\,px, three sky annuli, the flags as given or
grown by one pixel, with and without ensemble calibration, dropping each pair in turn, with the calibration floor
and with the three wavelength rules. We call a claim \emph{robust} if it holds in every check, \emph{sensitive} if in
at least half and \emph{inconclusive} otherwise. This is a fixed rule, not a probability.

\textbf{What we did not do, and why.} The SPHEREx images carry an empirical PSF per detector zone (HDU \texttt{EPSF}),
so forced PSF photometry is possible; Section~\ref{sec:epsf} tests it as a second measurement, and
Section~\ref{sec:psfsub} benchmarks PSF-matched and proper image subtraction \parencite{zackay2016} (not adopted). A real zero-point fit across epochs \parencite[ubercalibration,][]{padmanabhan2008} needs many stars per
image and larger cutouts. Learned real/bogus classifiers \parencite{duev2019,mahabal2019} and conformal $p$-values
\parencite{bates2023} need a large labelled or null set that three targets do not provide.

\section{Independent ePSF-based photometric verification}\label{sec:epsf}
\textbf{Status: IMPLEMENTED and EXPERIMENTAL.} Nothing in Sections~\ref{sec:val}--\ref{sec:rob} uses it; it lives on a
separate branch (\texttt{psf\_photometry.py}, \texttt{reproduce\_epsf.py}, results in \texttt{epsf\_check.json}).

\textbf{Method.} Each cutout carries the SPHEREx effective PSF as a $21\times21$ grid of zones, each a $33\times33$ table
oversampled $\times5$. We take the zone nearest the star in parent-detector pixels (cutout pixel $+1-$\texttt{CRPIX1A},
as in the IRSA tutorial), record its SHA-256, and fit amplitude $\times$ ePSF $+$ constant background to the $9\times9$
native pixels by weighted least squares ($1/\mathrm{VARIANCE}$). Nothing is reprojected. A flagged pixel within 1.5\,px
of the star refuses the fit, as in the aperture photometry. For stars with S/N $\ge20$ the position may move by up to
1\,px; fainter stars stay at the WCS position. When $\chi^2_\nu>1$ the error is scaled by $\sqrt{\chi^2_\nu}$.
The ePSF as stored fits RW~Aur better than the same table flipped in $x$ in 4 of 5 epochs tried.

\textbf{RW Aur (fitted as one unresolved source; the two stars are 1.6$''$ apart in Gaia DR3).} All 8 pairs are
\emph{compatible} under the rule fixed beforehand ($|z|<3$, discrepant at $|z|\ge5$, $z$ the difference of the
log-ratios over their combined error): the ePSF ratios are within 0--2\% of the aperture ratios ($|z|\le1.3$),
all 8 pairs are brighter later and every pair is $\ge5\sigma$ with the ePSF alone ($10$--$66\sigma$, lower than the
aperture values because of the $\chi^2$ scaling). The $r=2$\,px aperture holds a steady 96--98\% of the ePSF flux.
The fit is not perfect: $\chi^2_\nu=4$--18 at S/N $\approx300$, with residuals at the core
(Fig.~\ref{fig:epsf}), and the best position is 0.12--0.21\,px from the WCS position in every epoch.
\textbf{Why $\chi^2_\nu$ is high.} We checked each possible cause on the real pixels (Fig.~\ref{fig:epsfdiag}).
The ePSF normalisation is correct (fine-grid sum 1.000), its orientation is right (the best of all eight flips and
transposes; the next is $4.3\times$ worse in $\chi^2$), and neither the zone choice nor a sloped background matters
(median $\chi^2_\nu$ 7.37 vs 7.53 and 7.31). Two causes are identified: Gaia field stars ($G=15$--19) at the edge of the
box, and real extra sky structure in the RW~Aur field (blank-sky scatter 1.30$\times$ the VARIANCE plane, against
0.998 in the WISE~0855 field, so the plane itself is right). Fitting the Gaia stars and scaling the sky noise brings the
median to 3.2 (1.6--8.5). The rest is a core mismatch that changes sign between epochs and that we could not attribute;
the RW~Aur binary (0.27\,px) cannot be split. None of these fixes moves the 8 RW~Aur ratios by more than 2\% from the
aperture values, and the $\chi^2$-scaled errors (0.86\%) agree with a bootstrap (0.86\%) and with the measured
aperture-vs-ePSF scatter (0.75\%). Down-weighting the core with a model-error term lowers $\chi^2_\nu$ but biases the
ratios by up to 18\%, so we do not use it. We keep the ePSF fit as an experimental cross-check, not a production
measurement.

Both methods read the same photons, so their errors could be correlated. We measured this with SIMULATED stars
injected into the real WISE~0855 cutouts: the scatter of $\ln(F_\mathrm{ap}/F_\mathrm{ePSF})$ is 0.95 and 0.99 times
the quadrature error at S/N 10 and 50 (weak correlation), and 1.72 times at S/N 200, where an extra scatter appears,
probably from the star's position inside the pixel. Dividing $z$ by the strictest factor (0.95) leaves all 8 RW~Aur
pairs compatible ($|z|\le1.37$). The injected stars follow the ePSF exactly, so this test does not include the
model error seen in the real residuals.

\textbf{WISE 0855.} Fluxes are below S/N~5, so ratios are not compared (all 27 pairs \emph{inconclusive} by the rule).
The published claim is still checked: no pair reaches $5\sigma$ with the ePSF either (largest $3.1\sigma$; aperture
$1.9\sigma$). \textbf{Barnard's Star} is not fitted: no epoch is usable.

\textbf{SIMULATED tests on real backgrounds.} At 104 blank spots in the 37 usable WISE~0855 cutouts the fit with no star
gives amplitude/error with standard deviation 1.07 and no $|z|\ge3$, so the errors are about right and there were no
false detections. Stars injected at S/N~10 are recovered exactly on top of the blank fit (the model is linear); a
0.3\,px position error loses 7\% of the flux, which is why bright stars fit their position.

\textbf{Combined label (fixed rule).} SUPPORTED VARIABILITY needs a robust aperture claim of change, every pair
compatible and $\ge5\sigma$ with the ePSF; LIKELY ARTIFACT a $\ge5\sigma$ aperture change the ePSF contradicts;
INSUFFICIENT DATA no usable epoch; INCONCLUSIVE anything else. RW~Aur: \textbf{supported variability}. WISE~0855:
\textbf{inconclusive} (no change seen, at S/N too low to prove constancy). Barnard's Star: \textbf{insufficient data}.
These are labels from a rule, not probabilities.

\textbf{Not validated / future work.} Only one ePSF per epoch (no PSF variation inside the $9\times9$ box), a constant
background, no wavelength dependence within the channel; the correlation test above uses stars that match the ePSF exactly.

\subsection{PSF-matched subtraction and a joint fit of RW~Aur A and B (EXPERIMENTAL, not adopted)}\label{sec:psfsub}
\textbf{Subtraction.} On the legitimate pairs (8 RW~Aur, 27 WISE~0855) we compared our direct difference with a
Gaussian-kernel match, an Alard--Lupton-style kernel and two ZOGY variants \parencite{zackay2016}, using each epoch's
zone ePSF. ZOGY assumes white noise, known PSFs and no resampling covariance; none holds exactly for undersampled,
reprojected $6.15''$ cutouts, so one variant adds an interpolation noise term. The direct method reproduces every
published significance (largest difference $10^{-14}\sigma$). In the clean WISE~0855 field (254 blank spots) the ZOGY
variant with interpolation noise has a calibrated null (MAD-based width 0.97) and recovers 91\% of SIMULATED point sources
at $S/N=7$ ($n=158$), against 11\% for our aperture difference; most of the gain comes from weighting the pixels by the
PSF, not from matching the PSFs. In the RW~Aur field (88 blank spots) every method's null is miscalibrated near the bright
star and the PSF mismatch leaves tens of spurious $\ge5\sigma$ peaks. Under the acceptance rule fixed before the run, no
method is adopted; the RW~Aur and WISE~0855 conclusions hold under all of them
(\texttt{psfsub\_check.json}).

\textbf{Can SPHEREx split RW~Aur A and B?} No. B lies 0.24\,px ($1.52''$) from A. A joint multi-epoch ePSF fit with a
second component gives A--B flux correlations of $-0.92$ to $-0.997$ per epoch; the fitted B fraction runs from $-0.18$ to
1.14 between epochs and swings from 0 to $\ge0.8$ when the assumed positions move by $\pm0.15$\,px. In SIMULATED tests
with a 0.1\,px PSF error, a single star fitted as a pair and a true pair give B fractions only 0.7 of their scatter
apart, so the better $\chi^2$ of the pair model mostly absorbs ePSF shape error. Sharing one sky position across
epochs fits worse ($\chi^2_\nu$ 7.02 joint vs 3.55 per epoch, single source). The A+B ratios of the 8 published pairs
agree with the aperture ratios within 1.7\% in every model, so the published blended ratios stand
(\texttt{jointpsf\_check.json}, Fig.~\ref{fig:jointpsf}).

\section{Spectral calibration note}\label{sec:wavecal}
The pair rule of Section~\ref{sec:method} needs the wavelength and channel width at the target. All numbers in this report read them
from the \texttt{WCS-WAVE} lookup table carried by every spectral image and cutout: a $9\times9$ grid of control points
interpolated bilinearly. IRSA describes this table as approximate, meant for visualisation, and points science analyses
to the \texttt{CWAVE} and \texttt{CBAND} maps of the separate Spectral WCS calibration product, which give the central
wavelength and bandwidth of every detector pixel. Our matched pairs differ by 0.5--6.8\,nm against half-channel limits
of 8.5--11\,nm, and every conclusion holds with the rule tightened to a quarter of a channel (Section~\ref{sec:rob}), so a
small error in the lookup would have to be systematic between epochs to change a result. We check this directly below.

\subsection{CWAVE/CBAND audit}\label{sec:wavecal-audit}
% WAVECAL-RESULTS
% We report the audit side by side; the production code and published evidence are unchanged.
We downloaded the official SPHEREx Spectral WCS calibration (version \texttt{cal-swcs-v5-2026-191}, the same files
under DR1 and QR3) and read \texttt{CWAVE}/\texttt{CBAND} at the parent-detector pixel of each target. The audit found a
real problem in our pipeline: astropy applies the celestial SIP distortion terms to the alternate \texttt{W} WCS too, so
the production code reads the \texttt{WCS-WAVE} table at a shifted pixel. Read without SIP, the table agrees with
\texttt{CWAVE} to 0.3\,nm (median); with SIP, the median and maximum differences are 0.58/1.59\,nm for RW~Aur and
3.74/17.8\,nm for WISE~0855 (up to 0.48 of a channel width).

With calibrated wavelengths, no RW~Aur or Barnard decision changes: RW~Aur keeps the same 8 valid pairs, ratio
$\times2.56$ and $175\sigma$, and both stability claims hold. For WISE~0855, two pairs become ineligible and three become
eligible, giving 28 valid pairs instead of 27; the largest change is $2.05\sigma$ instead of $1.93\sigma$, so ``no
$\ge5\sigma$ change'' still holds. No published conclusion changes. We keep the published numbers as they are, for
reproducibility.

\textbf{Why SIP leaks into the wavelength.} The spectral WCS is \texttt{WAVE-TAB}, a $9\times9$ lookup table on parent
pixel coordinates. The SIP keywords (\texttt{A\_}, \texttt{B\_}) carry no alternate-WCS letter and belong to the
celestial \texttt{TAN-SIP} solution, but astropy attaches them to any WCS key it reads, including \texttt{W}, and
evaluates the polynomial around \texttt{CRPIXW}. On all 280 cached cutouts, the \texttt{W} WCS with SIP removed matches
the documented bilinear table read to $2\times10^{-12}$\,nm, and the legacy value equals that read at the SIP-shifted
pixel (shifts of 3--71\,px), so SIP explains the whole offset. IRSA's SPHEREx tutorials turn SIP off for the spectral
WCS, and the archive documentation recommends \texttt{CWAVE}/\texttt{CBAND} for science.

\textbf{Corrected mode.} The pipeline now takes a wavelength source: \texttt{wcs} (legacy, the published numbers,
reproduced bit for bit), \texttt{wcs\_nosip} (table without SIP; median 0.2--0.7\,nm from \texttt{CWAVE}) and
\texttt{cwave} (official maps at the parent pixel, with \texttt{CBAND} for the half-channel rule), which is the
scientifically correct choice. In \texttt{cwave} mode the stability audit gives RW~Aur 24/24 and 18/24, as published,
and WISE~0855 35/35. Details:
\path{submission/evidence/wavecal_check.json}, \path{backend/scripts/reproduce_wavecal.py}.

The same audit checked the ePSF tables in all 204 cached cutouts: detectors 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 use a $21\times21$ zone
grid and detector 3 an $11\times41$ grid, as documented. Our zone selection picks the zone containing the star for
both layouts.

\section{Comparison with IRSA's own SPHEREx tools}\label{sec:irsatools}
We ran two of IRSA's own tools on exactly our exposures (Fig.~\ref{fig:irsatools}). The script is
\texttt{compare\_irsa\_tools.py} and its results are in \texttt{irsa\_tools\_check.json}.

\paragraph{Source Discovery Tool (SDT).} We ran IRSA's tutorial package unmodified (irsa-tutorials commit
\texttt{8a83a9e3}) on fresh cutouts of the same exposures, matched by observation ID, and converted our fluxes to mJy
with the pixel solid-angle map. For RW~Aur at the same 2\,px aperture the per-exposure ratio SDT/ours is 0.9998
(robust scatter 0.0040, $n=10$): 0.9979 on exposures we measure on their own grid and 1.0055 on exposures we measure
after reprojection, so our bilinear reprojection costs about 0.8\% of the flux in a 2\,px aperture. On the 8 published
pairs SDT's brightness ratio is 1.0093 times ours (scatter 0.0037), and both call all 8 pairs changed. SDT's
significances are higher because its error omits the sky-level term that ours includes. Two differences are worth
knowing: SDT reads the solid-angle map at the detector corner rather than at the cutout's position (1.3\% low here),
and it reads the wavelength without the SIP terms ($+1.4$\,nm on D1, $+3.7$\,nm median on D6 relative to our legacy
values; Section~\ref{sec:wavecal-audit}). For WISE~0855 (our $S/N\approx3$) SDT/ours is 0.98 with scatter 0.22, and all
27 ``no change'' verdicts agree at 2\,px. At SDT's default 3\,px aperture it calls 3 pairs changed; each involves
flagged pixels 2--3\,px from the target that SDT sums and our 2\,px aperture never reaches.

\paragraph{Moving Object Search Tool (MOST).} For (216)~Kleopatra over MJD 60843.4--60846.7, MOST lists 36 SPHEREx
DR1 images and we used 38: 36 are common, none is MOST-only, and our 2 extra entries are one exposure on D3 and D6
where we had already found the asteroid beyond the detector edge and measured nothing. MOST's positions equal JPL
Horizons as seen from SPHEREx at exposure start (rms $0.001''$); against our measured centroids they differ by
$0.33''$ median ($n=31$), mostly that start-versus-mid-exposure offset, far below the $6.15''$ pixel.

\paragraph{Not run.} The SPHEREx Spectrophotometry Tool is available only inside the SPHEREx Data Explorer web
interface, with no documented scripting interface, so we did not run it.

\section{Moving objects}\label{sec:motion}
Section~\ref{sec:planetx} explains why a distant planet would appear in our comparisons, if at all, as a source present
in one epoch and absent or displaced in another. Our pipeline was designed for fixed targets and is not a
moving-object search; the check below only asks whether the validated comparison step flags such sources.

\subsection{Validation on a known asteroid: (216) Kleopatra}\label{sec:motion-check}
% Numbers below are from submission/evidence/motion_check.json (backend/scripts/reproduce_motion.py, ~16 min).
To test whether our detection, linking and astrometry work on a real moving body, we ran a blind search on 38 real
SPHEREx DR1 exposures (all six detectors, 0.83--5.0\,$\mu$m, MJD 60843.4--60846.7, seven visits) of a 0.19$^\circ$
field crossed by the main-belt asteroid (216)~Kleopatra ($V\approx12.8$, 2.9\,AU). This is a validation on a known
object, not a search for new ones.

\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Blind linking.} Of 1314 detections, 1248 were stationary. Linking returned two tracks; one is Kleopatra
(31 detections over 7 visits and 75\,h, median 0.38$''$ from the JPL Horizons prediction).
\item \textbf{Astrometry against Horizons.} We query Horizons as seen from SPHEREx itself (observer
\texttt{500@-163182}): from 650\,km altitude the orbital parallax shifts the asteroid by up to 3.3$''$ with a 98-minute
period. Residuals are 0.15$''$ (RA\,cos\,Dec) and 0.18$''$ (Dec) rms; against a geocentric ephemeris they rise to
1.34$''$ and 0.63$''$, so we resolve SPHEREx's own parallax. Field stars in the same cutouts give an astrometric floor
of 0.40$''$ per axis at S/N$\geq$20.
\item \textbf{Fitted motion.} $2.0767\pm0.0037$ and $4.3102\pm0.0037\,''$/h, versus 2.0771 and 4.3075 from Horizons
fitted the same way ($-0.12\sigma$, $+0.74\sigma$).
\item \textbf{Pipeline flag finding.} In 35 of 38 exposures the SPHEREx pipeline flags pixels on the asteroid as
OUTLIER, so our static-target mask erases it. The motion check keeps OUTLIER pixels and masks every other bad flag;
the static pipeline is unchanged.
\item \textbf{SIMULATED injection-recovery.} Fake movers added to the real cutouts are recovered in 75--100\% of trials
at S/N$\geq$7 and 2--15$''$/h, and 14--50\% at S/N\,5, as expected at a 5$\sigma$ threshold.
\item \textbf{False links.} Noise-only fields: no tracks in 10/10 runs. Real detections with shuffled epoch times:
at least one 3-visit track in 14/20 runs (18/20 without Kleopatra), none with 5 visits. A 3-visit track in this field
is therefore not evidence of a real mover.
\end{itemize}

The second track (6 detections, 5 visits) is not a moving object. Its six detections sit at only three fixed sky
positions, which an order-2 fit passes through exactly (a straight line gives rms 4.0$''$, $\chi^2_\nu=28.8$). Two
positions are faint static stars, Gaia DR3 $G=18.4$ and 19.4 (also in AllWISE, within 1.3$''$), seen above 5$\sigma$
only in a few short-wavelength exposures less than 4\,h apart, shorter than our static-source time span; forced
photometry over 19 exposures stacks to S/N $\approx15$ at each. The third is a single 4.9\,$\mu$m exposure, 4.5$\times$
sharper than a point source and absent from the other exposures of the same visit: most likely an unflagged detector
hit. JPL's small-body identification from SPHEREx finds no known object within 100$''$ (positive control: Kleopatra
at 0.017$''$). We added a degenerate-track check that flags this case and passes Kleopatra; it is not yet part of
the published run (\path{submission/evidence/motion_followup.json}). Further known-object cases ((2052)~Tamriko, (433)~Eros)
were identified but not run because IRSA returned HTTP 502 during the downloads.

One object, one 3.3-day window and slow motion only: this shows the method works on a real case, not survey-level
completeness. HIPPARCHUS has not found Planet X.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item Fluxes are relative (aperture sums of surface brightness). They are adequate for ratios at matched wavelength
but are not calibrated flux densities.
\item The SPHEREx PSF is undersampled; aperture photometry after bilinear reprojection carries a systematic error that
the formal $\sigma$ does not include. The control star bounds it to roughly 15\% and the field stars to 12.5\%
(Section~\ref{sec:rob}). The 175$\sigma$ figure is therefore a formal value; the size of the change ($\times2.5$) is
the robust result.
\item The result uses one control star, and not every pair has one; the field-star check has only 0--2 stars per cutout. Target positions are approximate priors checked
against catalogues only after the analysis. SPHEREx pixels are $6.15''$, so the RW~Aur measurement is of both
components of the binary together, not of one star.
\item Injection-recovery inserts stars after reprojection and without their own photon noise; it measures the
detection threshold of the comparison step, not of the full pipeline.
\item We did not build a machine-learning ranker or a Bayesian model of candidates: with three targets and no
labelled set of real and false changes there is nothing to train or test such a model on honestly. A simple
$5\sigma$ threshold with a control star is the baseline any such model would have to beat.
\item The brightening of RW Aur is consistent with its known variability. It is a measurement from SPHEREx data, not a
discovery, and should be confirmed independently, for example against other surveys.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusions}
HIPPARCHUS turns ``compare these two SPHEREx images'' into a checked procedure: it matches wavelength across LVF
epochs, removes zodiacal light, honours pipeline flags, quantifies uncertainty, checks calibration on a control star,
and refuses when the data cannot support an answer. On real data it measures a strong, self-consistent brightening of
RW~Aur, refuses saturated Barnard's Star, and reports no detectable change for faint WISE~0855.
